# Urban canopy cover lowers surface temperature in dense neighbourhoods

## Abstract

Urban tree canopy is widely promoted as a heat-mitigation measure, but its effect in medium-sized cities remains poorly quantified. Here we combine airborne lidar, mobile transects and a radiative-transfer model to measure how canopy cover, canopy height and street orientation relate to daytime surface temperature across 48 neighbourhoods. Surface temperature varied by 6.2 °C across sites, and canopy cover was the strongest predictor: each 10% increase lowered surface temperature by 1.8 °C after we adjusted for building density and street orientation. The effect was larger in dense neighbourhoods, where the same increase lowered temperature by 2.4 °C, and it held in both morning and afternoon transects [1]. Canopy height had a weaker and less consistent effect, and dense neighbourhoods, which have less private green space, may rely more on public canopy for shade. These results point to canopy expansion as a priority in dense neighbourhoods, although the cross-sectional design cannot support causal claims. We measured only daytime surface temperature, so the estimates do not capture air temperature or humidity. The approach should transfer to other medium-sized cities with comparable lidar coverage.

## Introduction

Rapid urbanisation has intensified surface heat stress, and vegetation is one of the few levers available to planners [2]. Most evidence comes from megacities [3], and whether those findings generalise to medium-sized cities is unclear, because land-cover heterogeneity and rapid construction produce thermal gradients that coarse remote-sensing products resolve poorly [4]. Studies also differ in method [5], which makes them hard to compare. Few have considered canopy coverage, canopy height and street orientation together, and none has done so across a dense network of mobile transects in a medium-sized city. We address these gaps across 48 neighbourhoods, using airborne lidar, mobile transects and a coupled radiative-transfer model.

## Results

Canopy coverage ranged from 4% to 38%, and daytime surface temperature varied by 6.2 °C across the 48 neighbourhoods. Coverage was the strongest predictor: each 10% increase lowered surface temperature by 1.8 °C after we adjusted for building density and street orientation, whereas canopy height had a weaker and less consistent effect across both morning and afternoon transects. The effect was larger in high-density neighbourhoods, where a 10% increase lowered temperature by 2.4 °C [6]. We therefore read canopy cover as the dominant vegetation variable in this setting, although the cross-sectional design cannot support causal claims [7].

## Discussion

Our results agree with work in megacities [8], and they extend it to a medium-sized urban system, where the larger effect in dense neighbourhoods is plausible: those areas have less private green space and more heat storage, so public canopy may substitute for private shade [9]. Three limits qualify this reading. First, we measured daytime surface temperature rather than air temperature or humidity, and second, the design is cross-sectional, so unobserved site characteristics cannot be ruled out [10]. Third, our lidar coverage is from a single season. These limits do not overturn the main pattern, but they narrow the claim: canopy cover is linked to lower surface temperature, and dense neighbourhoods show the largest link. Whether expansion produces the same drop over time is a question for studies that track sites before and after planting.

## Methods

Canopy coverage and canopy height were derived from airborne lidar acquired in July 2023 at 1 m resolution. Surface temperature was measured with a calibrated thermal camera along 48 mobile transects, and a coupled radiative-transfer model was used to estimate how much shading and evapotranspiration each contributed. Neighbourhoods were then stratified by building density and street orientation.
