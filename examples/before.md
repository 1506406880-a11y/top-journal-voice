# A Comprehensive Investigation of the Pivotal Role of Urban Canopy in Mitigating Surface Heat

## Abstract

In the context of rapidly intensifying urban heat stress, the pivotal role of urban tree canopy in regulating surface temperature has attracted considerable attention; however, a comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms through which canopy structure modulates microscale thermal dynamics remains elusive, and this gap is particularly pronounced in medium-sized cities where rapid expansion and heterogeneous land cover create complex and poorly characterised thermal gradients that are not adequately captured by existing modelling frameworks, which tend to rely on coarse-resolution remote sensing products and therefore fail to represent the fine-scale interactions between canopy geometry, street orientation, and diurnal heat storage that ultimately determine pedestrian-level thermal exposure. In this study, we develop an integrated analytical framework that combines high-resolution airborne lidar, mobile transect measurements, and a coupled radiative-transfer model to quantify the relationship between canopy coverage, canopy height, and surface temperature across 48 neighbourhoods, and we further examine how these relationships vary as a function of street orientation and building density, thereby providing what we believe to be the first comprehensive assessment of canopy–heat interactions in this urban context. Our results demonstrate that canopy coverage is the dominant driver of surface temperature, with each 10% increase in coverage associated with a 1.8 °C reduction in daytime surface temperature, and that this effect is substantially amplified in high-density neighbourhoods, underscoring the pivotal importance of targeted canopy expansion for urban heat mitigation and suggesting that urban greening policies should prioritise dense, low-canopy neighbourhoods. Furthermore, our findings highlight the critical need for integrated planning frameworks that align canopy expansion with broader urban sustainability goals, and they provide a robust evidence base for policymakers seeking to design effective and equitable heat adaptation strategies in rapidly growing cities.

## Introduction

Rapid urbanisation has intensified surface heat stress across cities of the Global South, and a growing body of literature [1] has examined the role of vegetation in modulating urban microclimate, yet the mechanisms through which canopy structure influences pedestrian-level thermal exposure remain insufficiently understood, particularly in medium-sized cities where land-cover heterogeneity and rapid construction create thermal gradients that are difficult to characterise with conventional remote-sensing approaches [2]. Moreover, existing studies have predominantly focused on megacities, and the extent to which their findings generalise to smaller urban systems is unclear. Furthermore, methodological differences across studies [3] complicate comparison, and few investigations have simultaneously considered canopy coverage, canopy height, and street orientation in a single analytical framework. In this study, we address these gaps by combining airborne lidar, mobile transects, and a coupled radiative-transfer model across 48 neighbourhoods.

## Results

Across the 48 neighbourhoods, canopy coverage ranged from 4% to 38%, and surface temperature varied by 6.2 °C during the daytime. Canopy coverage was strongly associated with surface temperature, and each 10% increase in coverage corresponded to a 1.8 °C reduction, while canopy height had a weaker and less consistent effect. These findings demonstrate that canopy coverage is the dominant driver of surface temperature. The effect was amplified in high-density neighbourhoods, where the same increase in coverage produced a 2.4 °C reduction, and this amplification was consistent across morning and afternoon transects. These results clearly show that dense neighbourhoods benefit most from canopy expansion.

## Discussion

The results of this study provide robust evidence that canopy expansion constitutes the most effective strategy for urban heat mitigation, and they underscore the pivotal role of vegetation in shaping thermal exposure. Our findings are consistent with previous work [4], and they extend it by demonstrating that the effect is amplified in dense neighbourhoods. It is important to note, however, that our analysis is limited to daytime surface temperature, and that we did not measure air temperature or humidity. Furthermore, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Nevertheless, the consistency of the observed pattern across multiple transects suggests that the relationship is robust.

## Methods

Airborne lidar data were acquired in July 2023 and were processed to derive canopy coverage and canopy height at 1 m resolution. Mobile transects were conducted along 48 routes, and surface temperature was measured with a calibrated thermal camera. A coupled radiative-transfer model was used to estimate the contribution of canopy shading and evapotranspiration. Neighbourhoods were stratified by building density and street orientation.
